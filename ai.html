Security tools, each with its limit

  • The signed-message door (BREAK IT): Verifies every message on its raw bytes — Ed25519 signature, counter, exact layout — before anything parses it. Forgeries are refused and remembered by a living immune system; an independent referee keeps score. Limit: A signed-message gateway. Putting it in front of a particular MCP server is the deployment work, not a packaged proxy yet. Source
  • Liveware: A whole living system you verify in your own browser in ten minutes, including a self-defending door under a sealed attack test you re-run yourself. Limit: A demonstration system you can re-run end to end; it is not your production stack. Source
  • ScopeGuard: Flags an AI coding agent that skipped, loosened, deleted or neutered tests, told CI to ignore failures, or edited files outside its task — each with its line. CLI, GitHub Action and a Claude Code hook. Limit: Judges the honesty of a diff’s tests and scope. It is not a full static analyser or a software bill of materials. Source
  • The mutation gate (witness): Breaks your code on purpose, one small change at a time, and fails unless your tests notice every break. Limit: Proves the tests notice changes; it does not prove the code is right for a case nobody tested. Source
  • GlyphGuard: Finds invisible characters, homoglyphs, bidi overrides, hidden Unicode-tag payloads (ASCII smuggling) and fullwidth evasion in text an agent is about to read. Limit: Finds the characters and names them; deciding intent stays with you. Source
  • Conform: Checks AI structured output against your JSON Schema: PASS or FAIL, with the exact path of every violation. Limit: Checks the schema you give it; a wrong value that is still valid passes. Source
  • Canon: Proves two JSON documents are byte-identical after canonical form (RFC 8785-aligned), or shows exactly where they diverge. Limit: Proves two documents are the same; not that either one is true. Source
  • CiteLock: Checks that a claimed quote is verbatim in the source: VERIFIED, NEAR MATCH or NOT FOUND, with a receipt anyone can recompute. Limit: Exact text only; a faithful paraphrase is reported, not confirmed. Source
  • Shingle: Near-duplicate detection (word shingling and MinHash) that catches disguised copies, with a verdict you can re-derive. Limit: Measures similar wording, not similar meaning. Source
  • Behavioural Entropy Signatures, inward: Catches a hijacked or scripted session by how it behaves, even when the credential is valid. After Gary W. Floyd’s Behavioral Entropy Signatures. Limit: Learns each identity’s normal behaviour first; it is a behavioural signal, not proof. Source
  • Perimeter Post: An operator-issued capability — who may do what, within what budget, until when — verified before anything is acted on. Limit: A capability checkpoint for one hub. A broker issuing short-lived credentials across many services is the next build. Source
  • IsolationGate: Blocks the differencing attack: two released totals that subtract to one person’s data. Limit: Built on the membership of the released sets; it is not differential privacy. Source
  • Sovereign by architecture: Every tool runs on your device — in your browser, or on servers you run — and none sends what you give it to us or to a model provider. Open any tool page with your browser’s Network tab and watch: the only requests are for the page’s own files. Our own front door shows a live network ledger of what it fetched and what it sent — nothing is sent up. Limit: An architecture property you can watch, not a hardware enclave (TEE or SGX). Source
  • AlertTriage: Turns a flood of Wazuh, Suricata or Elastic alerts into a short ranked list of incidents, on your own machine, with no model judging. On a public attack data set the first group was an attack in all eight runs, and CI re-derives every published number from that data. Limit: Fewer of its top ten groups are attack than in a severity-sorted queue, because it folds an attack into one incident; it misses DNS exfiltration in that data. Source
  • IR Helper: Refuses to start without an authorization naming the host, collects read-only evidence, finds the tell-tale facts and proposes each step with its exact command and undo. A person approves and runs each one, and every step goes into a hash-chained log. Limit: Ten rules on Linux and Windows: it is not an EDR, and its silence is not an all-clear. Source

Why now, with sources

  • AI chats and legal privilege. In United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y., No. 25-cr-00503, February 2026) the court held that a defendant's own conversations with a consumer AI assistant were protected by neither attorney-client privilege nor the work-product doctrine. The opinion left open whether use directed by counsel would be treated differently. This is information, not legal advice. Source
  • EU AI Act logging. The EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to keep automatic logs of what they did (Article 12). The 2026 Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force from 27 July 2026, moved the high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 for stand-alone systems and 2 August 2028 for systems built into regulated products. Source
  • Small models for agent work. NVIDIA Research (Belcak et al., 2025) argue that small language models are powerful enough for, better suited to and more economical for many of the calls in agent systems. Source
  • The cost of owning inference. Lenovo's own cost paper (LP2368, 2026 edition: a hardware vendor's analysis) puts owned inference at up to 6 times cheaper per million tokens than cloud GPU instances and up to 17 times cheaper than hosted model APIs, for sustained high-utilisation work. Its own break-even case needs the system busy about 5.3 hours a day; below that, renting can cost less. Source
  • Model collapse. Shumailov et al. (Nature, 2024) show that training models on model-generated content makes the tails of the original data disappear. Source
